# Accounts held by or about minors: consent, GDPR and design rules for self-service sign-up (EU and France), November 2026

Research note for enlace_auth, the auth layer of a small self-hosted multi-app platform run by one individual in France. The upcoming consumers are an educational maths-competition practice app used by children aged about 10 to 13 with their parents and teachers, a judo technique learning game that club members (some of them children) may use, and a camera and gesture music instrument aimed at the education market that may later ship on iOS and Android. All are free today and may charge later. Sources were read on 2026-11-08. **This is not legal advice.** Items marked **[lawyer]** need a lawyer's confirmation before anything is built on them.

It builds on an in-house legal-posture record written for a parent-built school tool, which established that the GDPR household exemption never covers the operator; that France's digital-consent age is 15 under article 45 of the loi Informatique et Libertés (LIL); that this age matters only where consent is the lawful basis for a service offered to the child; the pattern "adults are the account holders, children are data, never users"; and that a minor's image right is held jointly by both parents (loi n° 2024-120). Those points are verified and extended here, not restated.

## Summary and recommendation

1. **Do not use consent as the lawful basis for the core service.** Base the account on an adult relationship: a contract with the parent for a family account, the school's public-interest task for a class (with the operator as the school's processor), and the club's membership for its members. GDPR Art. 8 and LIL art. 45 then do not apply to the core service, because both bite only "where consent applies" for information society services offered directly to a child [1][2][3]. Keep consent, given jointly by a parent and a child under 15, for optional extras only (a public leaderboard, sharing, camera recordings).
2. **No child signs up alone below 15.** CNIL's position is that minors can be treated as able to contract alone for an online service that processes their data only from 15, and that below 15 the parents conclude the contract [4][5]. A self-declared under-15 is sent to "ask a parent or teacher", and none of the child's entries is stored.
3. **Children never have an email, a birthdate or a free-text field.** They get a pseudonymous profile under an adult account or a class, with a school grade band instead of a birthdate. An age screen stores only the yes/no result [6].
4. **Treat a teacher-run class as the school's processing.** When a teacher uses the app with pupils, the Ministry of Education (or the head of a secondary school) is the controller, the operator is a processor, and an Art. 28 contract is owed [7][8]. Build the processor posture (no reuse, deletion at year end) before courting schools.
5. **Write a short DPIA for the maths and music apps.** Children are "vulnerable data subjects", and performance scoring or camera input supplies the second EDPB criterion [9][7].
6. **For the platform, the near-term work is legal pages plus the data model:** mentions légales (now LCEN art. 1-1, not art. 6), a privacy notice with a child-friendly version, CGU, and no non-exempt trackers. Today the DSA's minors article does not reach a micro-enterprise, and the 2023 French "majorité numérique" law has never entered into force [10][11][12].

## 1. The rules, with dates

**GDPR Art. 8 (applicable since 25 May 2018).** Where processing rests on consent "in relation to the offer of information society services directly to a child", a child under 16 (or the national age, never below 13) needs consent "given or authorised by the holder of parental responsibility", and the controller "shall make reasonable efforts to verify" it, "taking into consideration available technology" [1]. The EDPB reads it as cumulative: Art. 8 applies only when the service is an information society service offered directly to a child **and** the basis is consent [3] (§127). A service that "makes it clear… it is only offering its service to persons aged 18 or over", if nothing contradicts that, is not offered directly to a child [3] (§130). Art. 8(3) leaves national contract law untouched, and the EDPB adds that "both legal regimes may apply simultaneously" [3] (§151). Related articles: Art. 12(1) requires information "addressed specifically to a child" to be in clear and plain language, Art. 17(1)(f) gives a reinforced right to erasure for data collected under Art. 8, and Recital 38 singles out marketing and profiling of children [1].

**Is a free, non-commercial site an "information society service"?** The definition requires a service "normally provided for remuneration" [13]. The CJEU has held that remuneration need not come from the recipient: a free service paid for by advertising qualifies [14]. Whether a wholly free service with no advertising qualifies has no case law that we found. **[lawyer]** Design as if it does: the platform may charge later, and the safe design costs nothing extra.

**France, LIL art. 45 (current wording in force since 1 June 2019, renumbered from art. 7-1 by ordonnance n° 2018-1125).** A minor "peut consentir seul… à compter de l'âge de quinze ans". Below 15 the processing is lawful only if consent "est donné conjointement par le mineur concerné et le ou les titulaires de l'autorité parentale". The controller must write the information "en des termes clairs et simples, aisément compréhensibles par le mineur" [2]. The joint form (child **and** parent) goes further than GDPR's "given or authorised". CNIL's reading is that one parent's consent is enough, as an "acte usuel" of parental authority, but the other parent "doit être mis en capacité d'exprimer son opposition", and the parents cannot override the child's refusal [4].

**Contract instead of consent.** CNIL's Recommendation 4 says joint consent is not needed for processing that follows from an online contract: "soit le mineur peut conclure lui-même un tel contrat, soit les parents" conclude it [4]. Recommendation 1 sets the boundary. Minors are in principle "juridiquement incapables de conclure un contrat". CNIL considers it coherent, subject to the courts, to treat them as able to contract for services that process their data from 15, provided the service is adapted to them, minimised and clearly explained, and parents can ask for the account to be deleted [5]. The statutory hook is Code civil art. 1148 (in force since 1 October 2016): an incapable person may perform alone "les actes courants autorisés par la loi ou l'usage" [15]. Whether a 12-year-old's sign-up to a free learning app is an acte courant is decided case by case by judges [5]. **[lawyer]** Under Art. 6(1)(b), processing must also be genuinely *necessary* for the contract [16]: a leaderboard or analytics is not.

**Legitimate interest (Art. 6(1)(f)).** The article itself demands a careful balance "in particular where the data subject is a child" [1]. The EDPB says children's interests "will very often outweigh" the controller's, that in a conflict they "should in general prevail", and that profiling and targeted advertising "will generally not align" with the protection owed [17] (§§92-96; version 1.0 of 8 October 2024 is the latest the EDPB publishes as adopted). Legitimate interest fits narrow ancillary processing (security logs, abuse prevention), not the core relationship.

**CNIL's eight recommendations (June 2021) and later guidance.** The series [18] includes Rec. 1 (capacity), Rec. 4 (parental consent under 15) [4], Rec. 6 (privacy by design for minors: simple short information, no pre-selected age ranges that invite lying, optional features such as geolocation off by default) [19] and Rec. 7 (age and parental-consent verification) [20]. Rec. 7 calls verification an obligation of means ("efforts raisonnables") that must be proportionate to the risk. It warns that self-declaration and email checks are easily bypassed, calls facial recognition "disproportionné", and says that for risky processing "devrait… être exclu le recours à un système purement déclaratif" [20]. CNIL's age-verification analysis (July 2022, English version September 2022) found no method that is reliable, comprehensive and privacy-protective at once. It accepts card checks only as a transitional measure, rejects direct collection of ID documents, prefers a third party with "double anonymity", and warns against "disproportionately increas[ing]" age checks [21]. The newest CNIL item found that bears on this work is its 24 August 2026 guidance on online tools in schools (see section 3) [7]. We found no 2024-2026 CNIL text specific to parental-consent verification. Arcom's référentiel for pornographic sites is outside this scope.

**EDPB Statement 1/2025 on age assurance (11 February 2025, v1.2 April 2025).** Use the least intrusive effective method, and collect only the "age-related attribute" needed, for example "whether the user is over or under an age threshold". Checking every user's age for content "suitable for all audiences and devoid of any risk, would not pass the necessity and proportionality tests" [6] (§§13-14, 19-20).

**Loi n° 2023-566 du 7 juillet 2023 ("majorité numérique").** It would require social networks to obtain parental authorisation below 15, but its entry into force depends on a decree that was never issued. The Commission's August 2023 response found it in conflict with EU law (the TRIS notification procedure and the DSA). The government acknowledged in May 2025 that it "n'a pas pu prospérer" [11][22]. Its successor, the "loi visant à protéger les mineurs des risques auxquels les expose l'utilisation des réseaux sociaux", was adopted on 21 July 2026, but its article 1, the under-15 social-network ban, was struck down in **décision n° 2026-911 DC of 14 August 2026**. The Conseil constitutionnel found the blanket ban a disproportionate restriction of freedom of expression, and found that the age checks it implied lacked privacy safeguards [12]. Neither law reaches a learning app with no social features. The decision is a strong French signal against blanket age verification.

**DSA Art. 28 (applicable since 17 February 2024) and the Commission's guidelines (14 July 2025, OJ C/2025/5519).** Art. 28(1) requires "online platforms accessible to minors" to ensure a high level of privacy, safety and security. Art. 28(3) says this does not oblige platforms to process additional personal data to find out who is a minor [10]. Two filters keep it away from this platform. First, an "online platform" is a hosting service that disseminates user content *to the public* (Art. 3(i)), which a practice app with private progress is not. Second, Art. 19 exempts micro and small enterprises (fewer than 50 staff and no more than €10M turnover) from the whole section containing Art. 28 [10][23]. The guidelines restate that exemption [24]. They are still the best design checklist: private-by-default accounts, no streaks, autoplay or push notifications by default, no loot boxes, and age estimation rather than verification for sub-18 minimum ages [24]. The Commission's age-verification blueprint (July 2025) is an EUDI-wallet-aligned app that proves "over 18" without revealing identity [25]. It does not help with the under-15 question here.

**UK Age Appropriate Design Code (context).** In force 2 September 2020, with conformance expected by 2 September 2021. Its 15 standards (best interests, high privacy by default, data minimisation, no nudges) apply to services "likely to be accessed by" UK children [26]. They are a good benchmark, binding only if UK children are targeted.

**US COPPA amended rule.** Published 22 April 2025, effective 23 June 2025, with general compliance due **22 April 2026** [27]. It adds separate verifiable parental consent before disclosing children's data to third parties, a written data-retention policy that rules out indefinite retention, text-message consent methods, a "mixed audience" definition, and biometric identifiers as personal information [28]. It applies to foreign operators whose services are "directed to children in the United States" [29]. That matters only if an English-language store listing reaches US children. Neutral age screens must not default to an age and must stop "back-buttoning" [29].

**App stores (only if the music app ships natively).** Apple's Kids Category (Guideline 1.3) bars links out and purchases except behind a parental gate, and bars third-party analytics and advertising except in narrow cases. Once in the category, an app must keep meeting its rules. Guideline 5.1.4 lets apps ask for a birthdate and parental contact "only for the purpose of complying with these statutes". It also says the parental gate "is generally not the same as" parental consent, and that apps outside the Kids Category may not use metadata implying children are the main audience [30]. Google Play's Families policy forbids transmitting advertising IDs and similar identifiers from children or unknown-age users, requires a neutral age screen for mixed audiences, requires "adult action before allowing child users to exchange personal information", and requires an accurate privacy policy and Data safety form [31]. Apple's Declared Age Range API returns an age band without a birthdate. Press reports say Texas's App Store Accountability Act has applied to Texas accounts since 4 June 2026 [32] (secondary source).

## 2. What "verifiable parental consent" means in practice for a small operator

The EDPB's standard is proportionality. "In low-risk cases, verification of parental responsibility via email may be sufficient", while high-risk cases call for more proof. Example 23 runs: the child states their age, the child is told a parent must consent and gives a parent's email, the service obtains consent by email with "reasonable steps" to confirm parental responsibility, and checks go further only on complaint [3] (§§136-142). Controllers "should avoid verification solutions which themselves involve excessive collection" [3] (§145).

| Method | Fits this platform? | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Parent creates and verifies their own adult account, then adds the child profile, with a ticked attestation of parental authority | **Yes, the default** | Collects only the parent's contact details, as the EDPB's proportionate approach suggests [3] (§136). This is how Khan Academy (under-13 accounts are created and owned by a parent or teacher) [33] and Google Family Link (France: 15+ to manage one's own account) [34] work. |
| Child enters a parent's email, and the parent confirms by link or code (Scratch's under-16 flow; the EDPB's Example 23) | Yes, as a fallback invite | Low risk only. Scratch lets an unconfirmed account create projects but not join the community [35]. |
| €0.01 bank transfer or card transaction | No, for a free low-risk service | The EDPB cites it for higher risk and asks for an alternative for people without bank accounts [3] (fn 68). CNIL says "free access should remain free" [21]. |
| ID document, facial matching | No | CNIL calls ID analysis unsatisfactory and facial recognition disproportionate [21][20]. |

**Record-keeping.** Art. 7(1) requires the controller to *demonstrate* consent [1], and Art. 8 adds the "reasonable efforts" record [3] (§144). Keep an append-only record of the account holder, the child profile, the method, the time, the version of the notice shown, the purposes ticked, and any withdrawal. Keep no IP address and no copy of the email body. How long to keep proof after withdrawal is not settled in the sources read. **[lawyer]**

**When the child turns 15.** Consent given by a parent stays valid until the child acts. From the age of digital consent the child can confirm, change or withdraw it, and "the controller must inform the child about this possibility" [3] (§§147-149). For contract-based accounts, CNIL's Rec. 1 suggests letting a 15-year-old take the profile over [5].

## 3. Design patterns in real education products

- **Parent-owned account, child profiles, no child email.** Khan Academy sends all mail for under-13s to the parent or teacher and blocks child accounts from adding personal information to their profile [33]. Google Family Link requires a parent account in the same country [34]. Duolingo gives under-13 learners private profiles and no ads, according to the EU's Better Internet for Kids guide [36].
- **Teacher-created class accounts with a code.** Scratch Teacher Accounts create student accounts in bulk, with usernames and passwords and no student email [35]. Google Classroom requires Workspace for Education accounts for children 13 and under, joined by a 6-8 character class code, and the *school* consents for Core Services [37]. **Pix Junior** (CM1-CM2) is the leanest French model: the pupil types the school's code, picks the class, then picks their first name, and the teacher opens a 4-hour session from Pix Orga [38]. In secondary schools, Pix links pupils through the GAR, the Ministry's access broker, which passes resource providers an anonymised identifier and only the data needed for access [39].
- **Club-managed members.** The French Judo federation's licence is taken online by the licensee or entered by the club, which validates it. For minors, the legal representative signs the health attestation, and clubs require parental authorisation at registration [40] (the federation document was read only in summary). The club is the controller of its member list. An app used "by the club" therefore inherits the club's relationship with families, and the operator becomes the club's processor.
- **Who is the controller in school use (the point most likely to be missed).** In CNIL's 2023 EdTech sandbox, when a primary-school teacher adopts an app on their own, even paying from their own pocket, "le ministère a la qualité de responsable de traitement", and the vendor is a processor [8]. CNIL's AI-in-schools FAQ says a teacher using such a tool does so under the responsibility of the DASEN (primary) or the head of school (secondary), and must inform them [41]. Its August 2026 guidance points school tools to the public-interest basis rather than consent, because consent under school authority is rarely free. It says a DPIA is probably needed in most cases, and that information must reach pupils and parents before processing starts and again each school year [7].
- **Age gate.** Neutral: no pre-filled value, no hint of the "right" answer (CNIL Rec. 6 [19]; FTC [29]), and the yes/no outcome is the only thing stored [6].
- **Minimisation and defaults.** Pseudonymous display names (Scratch warns against identifying usernames [35]), no free text, no photo, private by default, no public profile, no chat, optional features off by default [19][24].
- **Retention.** Accounts left unused carry breach risk and break storage limitation. The EDPB's December 2025 recommendations on mandatory e-commerce accounts make that point and favour a guest mode where an account is not necessary [42]. That text is for e-commerce, but the reasoning carries over.
- **Child-friendly notice.** Required by Art. 12(1) and LIL art. 45 al. 3 [1][2]. CNIL recommends short sentences, icons, and information shown at the moment of decision [19].
- **DPIA triggers.** WP248 (endorsed by the EDPB) lists nine criteria, and two usually mean a DPIA. "Vulnerable data subjects" covers children, and "evaluation or scoring" covers performance tracking [9]. CNIL's mandatory list adds biometrics in places with pupils [43]. A maths app that scores children meets two criteria. A camera app aimed at schools likely meets two as well (vulnerable data subjects plus innovative use).

## 4. Concrete rules for enlace_auth's sign-up

**Fields.** *Adult account holder:* email (verified), optional display name, attestation "I am an adult and hold parental authority over the children I add" (family) or "I teach this class" (class). *Child profile:* pseudonym (chosen from a generated list or free choice checked against a deny-list, never a real name by default), avatar from a fixed set, grade band (e.g. CM2, 6e), optional birth year only if the app needs the turn-15 transition. No email, phone, birthdate, gender, school name, photo, location or free-text bio. An app needing more must justify it in its own DPIA.

**Is an age question needed?** On an adult-only app, no age question is needed. Say "service for people aged 15 or over" in the CGU and on the sign-up page. The EDPB accepts that for a low-risk service [3] (§§130, 135). On a mixed app with open self-sign-up, use a neutral two-button screen ("I am 15 or older" / "I am under 15") and keep a session flag so the child cannot simply go back and change the answer [29]. Do not ask for a birthdate [6]. In family and class modes there is no age question: the adult attests.

**A self-declared under-15 at open sign-up.** Create nothing. Show a child-friendly page ("Ask a parent or your teacher to set you up"). Optionally take a parent's email to send a one-time invitation, as in the EDPB's Example 23 [3]. Store only that email and a token, and delete it after a short expiry (say 7 days) if nobody confirms. Nothing the child typed is kept.

**Modelling the parent link and consent.** `GuardianLink(holder_account, child_profile, attested_at, method, notice_version)`, plus an append-only `ConsentRecord(child_profile, purpose, granted_by=[holder, child], granted_at, withdrawn_at, notice_version)` for each optional purpose. A second holder of parental authority can be invited as a co-holder or can lodge an objection through a published address [4]. The contract-based core needs no ConsentRecord; it hangs off the CGU acceptance of the holder.

**Withdrawal and deletion cascade.** Withdrawing one optional purpose stops it and deletes that purpose's data. Deleting the holder account deletes every child profile not handed over. Deleting a profile removes its progress and its shares and grants (route this through `on_credentials_changed` for sessions). The child's own erasure request is honoured too (Art. 17(1)(f) [1]; CNIL encourages minors to exercise their rights [18]). Class profiles are deleted, or returned to the school, at the end of the school year or contract [7]. Inactive family accounts are purged after a stated period, with a warning email to the holder first [42].

**Cookies and trackers (LIL art. 82).** Authentication, security (the CSRF cookie) and remembering the user's consent choice are exempt from consent if used exclusively for those purposes [44]. Anything else needs prior consent. On pages children reach, the simplest rule is none at all: no third-party fonts, scripts or analytics.

**Legal pages.** *Mentions légales:* required. Since loi n° 2024-449 (SREN), the identification duty sits in **LCEN art. 1-1**, and the non-professional anonymity option is art. 1-1 II ("personnes éditant à titre non professionnel"…) [45][46]. The in-house record cites the pre-2024 art. 6, which is now outdated. Charging money ends non-professional status, and full identification is then due. *Politique de confidentialité:* required from the first account (Arts. 12-14 [1]), with a separate version in plain French for children [2]. *CGU:* no statute requires them for a free service, but they *are* the contract relied on under Art. 6(1)(b), so in this design they are needed. Paid offers later bring consumer-law pre-contract information **[lawyer]**.

## 5. Consumer types at a glance

|---|---|---|---|---|
| Parent-run kids app (e.g. maths practice at home) | Contract with the parent (Art. 6(1)(b)) [4][5] | Parent; child is a profile | Not for the core. Yes, joint with the child under 15, for each optional consent-based feature [2][4] | Parent signs up with verified email, attests parental authority, adds a pseudonymous profile with a grade band, and the child logs in on the parent's device or with a parent-set code |
| Teacher-run class | Public interest task of the school (Art. 6(1)(e)); operator is a **processor** [7][8] | The school or Ministry, through the teacher; pupils are class profiles | No (consent is a poor fit under school authority [7]); parents are *informed* | Teacher account, class with a join code and a time-boxed session, pupil picks a first name or pseudonym (Pix Junior pattern [38]), Art. 28 terms, deletion at year end. Later: GAR [39] |
| Club-run members (e.g. judo club) | The club's membership contract or legitimate interest; operator is the club's processor **[lawyer]** | The club, which issues member profiles, or parents as in row 1 | Not for the core if the club's membership contract covers it; joint consent for optional extras | Club admin invites families or issues codes; the parent confirms by email; no child email |
| General adult app | Contract with the user | The user | No; state "15+" (or 18+) and do not target children [3] | Verified-email sign-up and a neutral age screen only where children plausibly arrive |

## 6. What else matters

- **Image, video and biometrics.** For the camera app, keep video and landmarks on the device. A minor's image right is exercised jointly by both parents and the child is associated "selon son âge et son degré de maturité" (Code civil art. 372-1 as restored by loi n° 2024-120, published 20 February 2024) [47]. Hand and face landmarks become special-category biometric data if processed "for the purpose of uniquely identifying" someone (Arts. 4(14), 9) [1]. Amended COPPA counts a child's image and biometric identifiers as personal information [28].
- **Members in other EU states.** The age of digital consent varies by Member State, and a cross-border service may have to follow the child's country [3] (§131). Contract-based accounts sidestep this; optional consent features should apply the strictest threshold, which is 16.
- **School adoption is a processor business.** It needs an Art. 28 contract, no secondary use of pupil data, no advertising trackers [7][41], and in time GAR membership [39].
- **Monetisation changes four things at once:** LCEN status, consumer law, CNIL Rec. 1's "actes courants" analysis for purchases made by minors [5], and the EDPB's view that commercial interests rarely outweigh a child's [17].
- **Engagement design.** Streaks, push notifications and loot boxes are on the DSA guidelines' list to switch off by default even where the DSA does not bind [24]. COPPA's 2025 rulemaking left push notifications as an open concern [28].

## Needs a lawyer

1. Whether a wholly free, ad-free service is an "information society service" (Art. 8 and LIL art. 45 scope) [13][14].
2. Whether a parent-concluded CGU is a sound Art. 6(1)(b) basis for a child's learning profile, and how far a 10-13-year-old's "actes courants" capacity reaches [5][15][16].
3. The controller and processor split for club use, and whether a teacher using the app without the head of school's knowledge exposes the operator [8].
4. How long to keep proof of consent after withdrawal.
5. Consumer-law and LCEN obligations once any payment exists.
